Most systems maps share a blind spot. They show suppliers, customers, regulators and competitors, but they leave out the soil, water, pollinators and climate that every one of those relationships depends on. When the living world appears at all, it is usually as a resource or a risk, rarely as a stakeholder.

I wanted a tool that fixes this, and one that a team with no background in systems thinking or ecology could pick up in a two-hour workshop. The result is the **More-than-Human Systems Mapping Toolkit**: eleven printable pages with seven steps, a worked example on every page and a critical check at the end.

## How it was developed

The toolkit brings together three sources.

i) **Four questions from recent research.** The backbone comes from the chapter *Four Questions for Systemic More-Than-Human Design in Practice* by Anton Poikolainen Rosén, Yuxi Chen, Suzanna Törnroth, İdil Gaziulusoy and Tatu Marttila (2024) <span class="tt-cite">[<a href="#ref-3">3</a>]</span>. They argue that more-than-human design needs systems thinking that is concrete and action-oriented, yet stays open and critical. They organise it around four questions:

1. How are systemic relationships established?
2. How do we select who or what to consider in a system?
3. How can more-than-human systems be mapped?
4. How do designers intervene in systems?

Each step of the toolkit answers one of these questions.

ii) **A typology of nonhuman stakeholders.** Simply putting "include nature" into a systems map is too vague to act on. So, the toolkit uses Veselova and Gaziulusoy's (2021) <span class="tt-cite">[<a href="#ref-5">5</a>]</span> seven types of natural nonhuman stakeholders: individual organisms, single-species collectives, multispecies collectives, life processes, living systems, biogeochemical cycles and processes of the atmosphere. This turns an abstract intention into a checklist a team can actually work through.

iii) **A proven beginner's format.** The step-by-step worksheet structure you find here is inspired from Mike Pinder's *Systems Mapping Toolkit for Beginners* (Explorer Labs) <span class="tt-cite">[<a href="#ref-2">2</a>]</span>. It starts with a challenge, then sets a boundary, lists stakeholders, draws connections, finds loops and ends with intervention points. I kept that friendly, left-to-right flow and rebuilt the content around the four questions. I also added three things that classic systems maps lack: nonhuman stakeholders, a way to mark how strong your evidence is, and a final critical check.

## How to use it

**Who it's for:** product, innovation and strategy teams, policymakers, students and researchers. You don't need prior experience with systems thinking.

**What you need:** 90–120 minutes, three to six people, sticky notes or a digital whiteboard, and one concrete challenge you can describe in a sentence. If you can, invite someone with ecological or local knowledge, or bring evidence from them.

**The seven steps:**

1. **Frame the challenge and choose your lenses (Q1).** Write the challenge the way your organisation normally would. Then pick two or three lenses, such as ecological, material flows, social or time. Every lens makes some relationships visible and hides others. Finish by reframing the challenge as *How might we … so that people and the wider living world can thrive?*
2. **Make your cut: draw the boundary (Q2).** Boundaries are made, not found; research calls these choices *agential cuts* <span class="tt-cite">[<a href="#ref-3">3</a>]</span>. Decide what is inside, what sits at the edge and what is outside. Write down what you left out on purpose, and why.
3. **Identify who and what is in the system (Q2).** List the people and technologies involved, then work through the seven types of nonhuman stakeholders. One being can belong to several types at once.
4. **Map the relationships (Q3).** Turn stakeholders into variables, things that can go up or down. Then draw arrows: what leads to more or less of what? Mark each arrow as documented evidence, local observation or an assumption to test. Nonhumans can't join your workshop, so be honest about who is speaking for them.
5. **Find feedback loops (Q3).** Trace reinforcing and balancing loops. Look especially for *missing* loops: harms that never feed back to whoever causes them.
6. **Locate leverage points (Q4).** Sort possible interventions from shallow to deep, following Meadows (1999) <span class="tt-cite">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>: parameters, feedbacks, design and intent.
7. **Frame interventions (Q4).** Turn one or two leverage points into *How might we … so that …* statements. Then plan the smallest experiment that would tell you whether the idea works for people and for the living world.

**The critical check.** Before acting, the toolkit asks you to question your own map. What did the boxes and arrows oversimplify? What did they leave invisible? Could the map be used to justify harm? Would a different boundary tell a different story? If the answers change the picture, loop back. That is the method working, not failing.

## A worked example: the robot lawn mower

Every step page includes the same worked example: a robot lawn mower in a private garden. Framed in the usual way, the challenge is "make our mower more attractive to garden owners". Mapped more-than-humanly, the picture changes. The time lens raises night-time mowing, when hedgehogs are out foraging. Research shows that robot mowers can seriously injure hedgehogs <span class="tt-cite">[<a href="#ref-4">4</a>]</span>, and injured animals are often found many hours later. Nothing in the system tells the owner what happened. That missing feedback loop points to interventions that go deeper than a new feature. Examples are making daytime mowing the default, or changing the app's measure of success from "lawn done" to "garden alive".
