# But human-centred design does such a great job!

I am a researcher in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and I agree, it’s great! Human-centred design has fundamentally shaped how we design and interact with technology today. Let's take a look at HCI’s milestones.

In the 1960s, the computer mouse transformed how we interact with computers. Point, click, explore. A seemingly simple innovation that helped make computing accessible to millions.

In the 1980s, researchers envisioned ubiquitous computing: technology seamlessly embedded in everyday objects and environments. Today, think smartwatches, connected appliances and smart homes.

In the late 1990s, affective computing emerged, exploring how technology could recognise and respond to human emotions. Today, emotion-aware interfaces and personalised digital experiences are part of our everyday lives.

Through the lens of human-centred design, HCI developed once-radical ideas and has helped turn into products, services and experiences we now take for granted. And HCI has brought enormous benefits. It has helped businesses create products that are more usable, accessible and desirable.

# So, why is the research community shifting its focus?

Over the past decade, a growing number of HCI researchers have been exploring systems thinking and more-than-human design.

Why? Because by focusing so closely on the human user, we sometimes lose sight of the bigger picture: humans are part of interconnected social and ecological systems. We depend on them, affect them and are affected by them.

Take AI. We have largely designed intelligent systems around human convenience, productivity and personalisation. But what about the energy and water they consume? The materials and supply chains behind the hardware? The consequences for workers, communities and ecosystems?

These are not separate issues. They are interconnected parts of the same system.

And perhaps this is one of the blind spots of human-centred design: **we can create technology that works brilliantly for its users while overlooking the wider costs and consequences**.

HCI researchers are increasingly reckoning with this challenge through an emerging research direction: **More-than-Human Computer Interaction**.

More-than-human design explores how to design technology beyond the immediate needs of human users — taking into account other species, natural environments, materials, infrastructures and the relationships between them.

In short: bringing **systems thinking** into HCI and design.

# Why should businesses and innovators care?

(**A word of advice**: keep an eye on developments in HCI. What researchers explore today can shape the products, services and markets we take for granted ten years from now. 😉)

Looking beyond the immediate user can reveal business opportunities and vulnerabilities that conventional approaches overlook.

It can help organisations:
- **Anticipate risks**: Identify dependencies on natural resources, ecosystems and supply chains before they become business vulnerabilities.
- **Discover new opportunities**: Uncover unmet needs, emerging expectations and potential markets beyond existing customer groups.
- **Build resilience**: Rethink resource use, explore circular approaches and develop business models that are less dependent on vulnerable systems.
- **Create lasting value**: Develop solutions that benefit customers and businesses while accounting for the communities and environments on which they depend.

Does this mean human-centred design is becoming a thing of the past? Certainly not. Understanding people remains essential. But focusing on people alone can leave important questions unanswered.

We need both: technology designed for excellent human experiences and wider sustainability.

**The next evolution of design is about seeing the bigger picture.**

Do not miss out on what lies beyond the immediate user. It might be where your next unmet need, breakthrough product or business opportunity is hiding.
