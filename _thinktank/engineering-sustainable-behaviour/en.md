How can we design digital experiences that make sustainable behaviour easier, more meaningful and more desirable? Here are five guidelines drawn from Sustainable HCI (Human-Computer Interaction) research:

## 1. Make sustainability the default and easiest choice.

Make sustainable options easy to find, understand and select. Research on washing-machine interfaces shows that simple design interventions can significantly increase the selection of energy-saving programmes [1]. But account for real human needs: eco-options must also work for people who need quick or high-temperature cycles.

*Ask yourself: How can sustainable behaviour become the path of least resistance?*

## 2. Deliver value beyond sustainability.

Connect environmental benefits with things people already value. Second-hand platforms, for example, combine sustainability with affordability, uniqueness and the enjoyment of shopping. Move beyond superficial gamification (points, streaks and leaderboards) [2] towards meaningful experiences that support autonomy, competence and connection [3]. Explore Self-Determination Theory [3,4] and Value-Sensitive Design [5].

*Ask yourself: What makes this experience worthwhile beyond its sustainability benefits?*

## 3. Acknowledge, understand, and design around emotions.

Acknowledge and design for the emotional responses sustainability can evoke, from guilt and anxiety to feelings of sacrifice and restriction. Apply principles of Affective Design [7] to anticipate how these responses shape the user experience. Avoid repeatedly highlighting problems without offering solutions, as this can foster powerlessness [3] and disengagement [11]. Instead, pair challenges with achievable actions and messages of hope, optimism and success [12–15].

*Ask yourself: What emotions does each interaction evoke, and what do they encourage people to do next?*

## 4. Provide immediate and actionable pathways forward.

Awareness alone rarely translates into action [8]. Help people move from understanding a problem to feeling capable of addressing it [3] by pairing information with clear, achievable next steps. For example, rather than simply showing users the carbon footprint of their choices, suggest lower-impact alternatives, explain how to access them, or connect users with opportunities for collective action. Offer practical recommendations, opportunities to build skills and suitable alternatives that make taking action feel both possible and worthwhile.

*Ask yourself: Does this interaction educate only or also provide an actionable way forward?*

## 5. Design beyond the individual user.

Do not treat individual behaviour change as the solution to sustainability challenges. Consider the wider systems that shape people's choices and how different stakeholders—individuals, communities, businesses, governments and academia—can *contribute to* and *benefit from* change. For example, embedding a tool-sharing app in local libraries creates shared value: developers save on infrastructure, residents immediately trust the app, libraries strengthen their community role, and municipalities reduce consumption and waste. A win-win for all.

*Ask yourself: Who can contribute to and benefit from this solution?*

## Conclusion

These guidelines are a starting point, not a universal checklist. The next step is to look beyond human needs. **More-than-Human Design** [9,10] explores how technologies, products, services and sociotechnical systems can support the wellbeing, agency and flourishing of humans, other living beings and the ecosystems they are part of.

For innovators, this also means staying open to new perspectives, needs and opportunities. The next breakthrough might come from designing *with* nature rather than simply designing for people. I predict that more-than-human thinking will increasingly shape design practice and industry—not just as the next design hype, but as a necessary evolution beyond user-centred design.
